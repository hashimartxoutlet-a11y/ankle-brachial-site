Why is a Normal ABI Range Between 1.00 and 1.40?

Patients often wonder why an ankle-brachial index is not simply "1.00" if blood pressure were identical throughout the arterial tree. In healthy human cardiovascular physiology, systolic blood pressure measured at the ankle is naturally 10 to 15 mmHg higher than systolic pressure measured at the brachial artery of the upper arm when the patient is resting in a supine (flat) position.

This hemodynamic feature occurs due to pulse pressure amplification and peripheral wave reflection. As the left ventricle ejects blood into the elastic ascending aorta, pressure waves travel distally toward the muscular arteries of the pelvis and lower legs. The tapering vessel caliber and higher vascular tone of distal arteries cause pressure waves to reflect back, summing together to generate a higher systolic peak at the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries.

Consequently, a ratio between 1.00 and 1.40 reflects unobstructed lower-extremity arterial conductance and physiological wave propagation.

Comprehensive ABI Diagnostic Category Matrix

Classification Index Range Underlying Hemodynamics Typical Patient Symptoms
Normal Range 1.00 – 1.40 Preserved distal arterial pressure; no hemodynamically significant stenosis. Asymptomatic; normal walking endurance without exertional calf tightness.
Borderline 0.91 – 0.99 Equivocal pressure reduction; mild flow impedance that may worsen under stress. Often asymptomatic at rest; may notice mild calf ache during rapid or uphill walking.
Mild-to-Moderate PAD 0.41 – 0.90 Significant arterial lumen narrowing (stenosis > 50%) in femoral, popliteal, or iliac vessels. Classic intermittent claudication (predictable cramping in calf, thigh, or buttock relieved by rest).
Severe Ischemia 0.00 – 0.40 Severe multi-level arterial occlusion; perfusion insufficient even for basal cellular metabolism. Rest pain (severe burning pain in foot when elevated in bed), non-healing ulcers, gangrene.
Non-Compressible > 1.40 Severe arterial wall calcification prevents cuff occlusion. True pressure cannot be determined. Common in diabetes and renal failure; high risk of "masked" underlying ischemia.

Understanding Borderline ABI (0.91 to 0.99)

A resting ABI between 0.91 and 0.99 represents a clinical "gray zone." While not meeting the formal diagnostic threshold for peripheral artery disease (which is strictly defined as an ABI ≤ 0.90), large epidemiological studies such as the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) have shown that patients with borderline ABI scores experience a significantly higher 10-year risk of cardiovascular events and mobility decline compared to those with an index above 1.00.

When a patient presents with classic exertional leg pain but a resting ABI of 0.93, clinicians frequently order a post-exercise treadmill ABI test. The patient walks on a motorized treadmill (standardized at 2 miles per hour with a 10% to 12% grade) for up to 5 minutes. In healthy individuals, exercise increases blood flow and maintains or increases the ABI. In a patient with occult arterial stenosis, distal arterioles dilate fully during exercise, precipitating a dramatic drop in ankle systolic pressure (an ABI decrease > 20% or ankle pressure drop > 30 mmHg), which unmasks the diagnosis of PAD.

Why ABI Over 1.40 is Not a Sign of "Super Healthy" Arteries

It is a common misunderstanding among patients to assume that if an ABI of 1.10 is good, an ABI of 1.55 must be even better. In vascular medicine, any ABI above 1.40 is considered abnormal and uninterpretable.

An elevated ABI occurs because the tunica media of the tibial arteries has become densely calcified—a condition known as Mönckeberg's medial calcific sclerosis. When the clinician inflates the pneumatic cuff around the ankle to 200 or 250 mmHg, the calcified vessel acts like a rigid lead pipe, resisting physical collapse. The ultrasound Doppler continues to detect arterial flow simply because the cuff cannot pinch the stiffened artery shut.

When this occurs, clinicians must disregard the ABI and immediately proceed to secondary vascular assessments, principally the Toe-Brachial Index (TBI) or transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO2) measurements. Learn more in our dedicated guide: Why ABI Fails in Diabetic Patients.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is considered a normal ABI score? ↓

Under American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology (ACC) clinical practice guidelines, a resting ABI score between 1.00 and 1.40 is considered normal, indicating healthy lower-extremity arterial perfusion.

Why is the normal ABI ratio greater than 1.0 instead of 1.0 exactly? ↓

In a healthy supine patient, systolic blood pressure is naturally 10 to 15 mmHg higher at the ankle than in the upper arm due to the physical phenomenon of arterial pulse pressure amplification and reflected pressure waves in more peripheral muscular arteries.

What does a borderline ABI between 0.91 and 0.99 mean? ↓

A borderline ABI represents an equivocal result. The patient may have early or mild arterial stenosis that is not fully apparent at rest. Clinical guidelines recommend a treadmill exercise ABI test or vascular ultrasound if the patient experiences walking pain (claudication).

What happens if the ABI is above 1.40? ↓

An ABI above 1.40 indicates incompressible or rigid arteries caused by medial arterial calcification (Mönckeberg's sclerosis), frequent in diabetes and renal failure. The test cannot diagnose PAD, and an alternative test such as a Toe-Brachial Index (TBI) must be ordered.
