How ABI Is Measured

An evidence-based overview of the continuous-wave Doppler examination protocol recommended by the American Heart Association.

Patient Preparation & Rest Period

To obtain physiologically accurate baseline arterial pressures, the patient must lie comfortably in a supine position (flat on their back) for 5 to 10 minutes in a calm, temperature-regulated clinical examination room prior to testing.

  • Smoking & Nicotine: Patients must avoid smoking or using tobacco/nicotine products for at least 2 hours before the test. Nicotine causes acute peripheral vasoconstriction that artificially raises ankle resistance.
  • Caffeine & Exercise: Vigorous exercise and caffeine should be avoided prior to evaluation to prevent transient blood pressure spikes.
  • Room Temperature: Cold environments cause peripheral vasospasm (Raynaud phenomenon or shunting), which can obscure Doppler signals.

The 8 MHz Handheld Doppler Probe

A standard stethoscope auscultation technique cannot detect faint or low-amplitude pulses in diseased distal pedal arteries. Instead, clinicians utilize a handheld continuous-wave Doppler ultrasound probe (typically 8 MHz) paired with acoustic coupling gel. The probe is positioned at a 45° to 60° angle to the vessel axis against arterial blood flow to optimize acoustic signal clarity.

Brachial & Ankle Occlusion Sequence

Appropriately sized pneumatic blood pressure cuffs are wrapped snugly around both upper arms and both lower legs (2 to 3 cm above the ankle malleoli).

  1. Signal Detection: The Doppler probe is placed over the artery until a clear pulsatile acoustic signal is heard.
  2. Suprasystolic Inflation: The cuff is inflated until the arterial sound completely disappears, then inflated an additional 20–30 mmHg.
  3. Controlled Deflation: The cuff pressure is deflated slowly at approximately 2 mmHg per second.
  4. Systolic Recording: The exact pressure reading at which the very first Doppler flow sound returns is recorded as the systolic pressure.

Target Pedal Arteries: DP & PT

For each foot, systolic pressure is measured in two separate arterial beds:

Dorsalis Pedis (DP): Located on the top surface of the foot, running along the groove between the first and second metatarsal bones.
Posterior Tibial (PT): Located immediately posterior and inferior to the medial malleolus (inner ankle bone).

Key Clinical Terminology

Systolic Blood Pressure Peak arterial pressure exerted during cardiac ventricular contraction. Diastolic pressure is not utilized in ABI calculation.
Brachial Artery The primary arterial conduit of the upper arm, serving as the benchmark for central systemic perfusion pressure.
Dorsalis Pedis (DP) The main vessel across the dorsal bridge of the foot, continuing from the anterior tibial artery.
Posterior Tibial (PT) The posterior vessel coursing behind the inner ankle bone to supply the plantar surface and toes.

Abnormal Severity Sub-Bands (≤ 0.90)

0.71 – 0.90: Mild PAD

Patients often experience mild intermittent claudication after walking several blocks. Hemodynamic restriction is mild under resting conditions.

0.41 – 0.70: Moderate PAD

Frequently associated with claudication after walking short distances (e.g., 100–200 meters). Significant multilevel arterial stenosis is common.

≤ 0.40: Severe Ischemia

Reflects critical limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI). Patients may have ischemic rest pain (burning discomfort in toes/foot when lying down) or tissue ulcers.

Limits of ABI

When standard ankle-brachial index testing is insufficient and supplementary diagnostic modalities must be employed.

Arterial Calcification in Diabetes & CKD

In individuals with long-standing diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease (CKD), or advanced age, calcium accumulates in the middle muscular layer of the arterial wall (Mönckeberg's medial calcific sclerosis).

Because these rigid, "pipe-stem" arteries resist external cuff compression, standard pneumatic cuffs require excessive pressures or fail to occlude flow altogether, producing falsely elevated readings (>1.40). In such cases, an apparently high ABI can dangerously mask severe underlying PAD.

The Toe-Brachial Index (TBI) Alternative

Digital arteries of the great toe are rarely affected by medial calcific sclerosis. When the ankle index is greater than 1.40 or vessels are noncompressible, clinical guidelines mandate obtaining a Toe-Brachial Index (TBI).

Using a miniature 2.5 cm pneumatic digit cuff and an infrared photoplethysmography (PPG) optical sensor, systolic toe pressure is measured. A TBI ≤ 0.70 confirms a diagnosis of lower-extremity peripheral artery disease.

Normal Resting ABI with Exercise Symptoms

A resting ABI in the normal range (1.00–1.40) does not entirely rule out PAD. Patients with single-level or proximal stenosis (e.g., in the iliac or common femoral arteries) may receive adequate blood flow at rest through collateral channels.

During treadmill walking (typically 2 mph at a 10–12% incline), increased muscular demand triggers peripheral vasodilation; fixed proximal blockages cannot supply required flow, causing post-exercise ankle pressure to drop precipitously (>20 mmHg or >20% decline in ABI).

Microvascular Disease & Tissue Healing

ABI measures macrovascular pressure in named conduit arteries. It cannot evaluate capillary-level microcirculation, diabetic neuropathy, or microvascular shunting.

For patients with chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI) or deep non-healing foot ulcers, vascular surgeons rely on transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO2) or skin perfusion pressure (SPP) to assess local microcirculatory healing capacity.

When Is an ABI Test Recommended?

Guideline-recommended screening criteria, classic clinical symptoms, and urgent warning signs.

1

Exertional Leg Symptoms

Intermittent Claudication: Muscle cramping, tightness, aching, or extreme fatigue occurring reproducibly in the calf, thigh, or buttock during walking that subsides within 10 minutes of standing still.

Atypical symptoms such as leg heaviness or walking fatigue also warrant assessment.

2

Physical Exam & Trophic Signs

  • Diminished or absent pedal pulses (DP or PT).
  • Non-healing wounds or sores on toes, heels, or ankles.
  • One foot noticeably colder than the other.
  • Shiny skin, hair loss on the shins/feet, or thickened nails.
  • Bruits heard over the femoral or iliac arteries.
3

High-Risk Screening Cohorts

  • All adults aged 65 and older.
  • Adults aged 50–64 with diabetes, smoking history, high cholesterol, or hypertension.
  • Adults under 50 with diabetes and another cardiovascular risk factor.
  • Known atherosclerosis in coronary or carotid arteries.

Urgent Warning Signs Requiring Immediate Emergency Medical Evaluation

If you or someone in your care develops sudden severe leg pain, an icy cold limb, pale or bluish skin discoloration, loss of sensation (numbness), inability to move the foot or toes, or rapidly darkening or black necrotic tissue, seek emergency medical care immediately. These are clinical hallmarks of acute limb ischemia or critical limb loss requiring emergency vascular intervention.

Frequently Asked Questions

Clear, evidence-grounded answers to the most common clinical and diagnostic questions about the ankle-brachial index.

What is the ankle-brachial index (ABI)?

The ankle-brachial index (ABI) is an objective, non-invasive vascular screening test that compares systolic blood pressure measured at the ankle to systolic blood pressure in the upper arm (brachial artery). It evaluates arterial circulation in the legs to detect peripheral artery disease (PAD), where atherosclerotic plaque restricts lower-extremity blood flow.

What is a normal ABI range?

Under American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology (ACC) guidelines, 1.00 to 1.40 is normal. An index between 0.91 and 0.99 is borderline, 0.90 or below is abnormal and diagnostic for peripheral artery disease, and readings above 1.40 indicate noncompressible or calcified vessels requiring alternative testing.

Is ABI the same as ABPI?

Yes. ABPI stands for ankle-brachial pressure index, the terminology commonly used in the United Kingdom, Europe, and Australia. Both terms refer to the identical physiological calculation: systolic pressure at the ankle divided by the highest arm systolic pressure.

How do you calculate ABI?

Divide the higher of the dorsalis pedis (DP) or posterior tibial (PT) systolic pressure measured at one ankle by the highest systolic blood pressure measured between both arms. Repeat the division for the opposite leg. For example, if the ankle reading is 100 mmHg and the highest arm reading is 130 mmHg, the index is 100 ÷ 130 = 0.77.

Which ankle pressure should I use, DP or PT?

Clinical practice standards dictate using the higher of the two ankle pressures (DP or PT) for each individual leg. The higher reading represents the limb's most patent arterial conduit and best estimates true distal perfusion.

What does an ABI above 1.4 mean?

An ABI above 1.40 means the tibial and peroneal arteries are non-compressible due to severe vascular stiffening or medial arterial calcification (Mönckeberg's sclerosis), which is common in individuals with diabetes, kidney failure, or advanced age. Because the cuff cannot compress the vessel, the number is falsely high. Clinicians perform a Toe-Brachial Index (TBI) or duplex Doppler ultrasound instead.

Can I measure ABI at home?

Standard home blood pressure monitors designed for the upper arm cannot reliably measure ankle pressures. Accurate ABI assessment requires continuous-wave Doppler ultrasound equipment, specialized cuff sizing, and clinical training. You can use this calculator to compute and interpret readings provided by your medical team, but physical measurements must be performed in a clinical setting.

How should I prepare for an ABI test?

Wear comfortable, loose-fitting clothing that allows easy placement of blood pressure cuffs on both upper arms and lower legs. Avoid smoking or using nicotine for at least 2 hours before the examination, as nicotine causes arterial vasoconstriction. You will be asked to lie flat and rest quietly for 5 to 10 minutes prior to inflation.

What is a toe-brachial index and when is it used?

The toe-brachial index (TBI) is the ratio of systolic blood pressure measured in the great toe to the highest arm systolic pressure. Digital arteries of the toe are rarely affected by medial arterial calcification. It is specifically ordered when the ABI is above 1.40 or ankle arteries cannot be compressed. A TBI of ≤ 0.70 confirms PAD.

Does a normal ABI rule out PAD?

No. A resting ABI between 1.00 and 1.40 does not exclude PAD in patients with classic exertional calf cramping (claudication) or proximal arterial stenosis. In such patients, a treadmill exercise ABI test or vascular imaging is recommended, which demonstrates post-exercise pressure reduction.

When should I see a doctor?

Consult a physician if your calculated ABI is ≤ 0.90, borderline (0.91–0.99), or > 1.40, or if you experience leg cramping during walking, non-healing foot sores, or cold feet. Seek immediate emergency evaluation for sudden severe leg pain, numbness, a cold pale leg, or dark black skin discoloration.
