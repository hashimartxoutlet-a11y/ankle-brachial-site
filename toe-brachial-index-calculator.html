Understanding the Toe-Brachial Index (TBI)

The Toe-Brachial Index (TBI), also referenced in vascular laboratories as the toe-brachial pressure index (TBPI), is a specialized non-invasive physiological diagnostic measurement that evaluates arterial perfusion in the distal microcirculation of the lower extremity. While the standard Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) remains the first-line screening tool for peripheral artery disease (PAD) in the general population, the ABI suffers from a well-documented technical vulnerability: it relies on compressing medium-sized tibial and peroneal arteries at the ankle.

In patients where ankle vessels become calcified and rigid, an external pneumatic cuff cannot collapse the vessel lumen, producing an artificially elevated ABI (> 1.40) or falsely normal readings. The Toe-Brachial Index overcomes this limitation by evaluating the small digital arteries of the great toe, which lack the muscular tunica media that undergoes heavy calcification.

The Mathematical Formula for TBI

Clinicians calculate the toe-brachial index using the following standard physiological equation:

TBI = Highest Toe Systolic Blood Pressure (mmHg) ÷ Highest Arm Systolic Blood Pressure (mmHg)

Similar to standard ABI guidelines published by the American Heart Association (AHA), the denominator is always the higher of the two brachial artery pressures (left arm vs. right arm). The numerator is the systolic pressure obtained at the hallux (great toe). Under normal physiological conditions, toe pressure is approximately 20 to 30 mmHg lower than systemic brachial pressure, producing a normal baseline ratio of greater than 0.70.

Standard TBI Clinical Reference Categories

According to consensus statements from the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) and the American College of Cardiology (ACC), toe-brachial indices are interpreted across three primary diagnostic tiers:

TBI Value Classification Physiological Interpretation Recommended Clinical Action
> 0.70 Normal Range Normal digital arterial perfusion; adequate blood flow to the distal foot and toes. Routine preventive cardiovascular care and ongoing glycemic management if diabetic.
0.41 – 0.70 Reduced / Suggests PAD Mild to moderate lower-extremity arterial disease. Blood supply may be inadequate during exertion or tissue healing. Formal vascular consultation, duplex ultrasound imaging, aggressive medical optimization.
≤ 0.40 Severely Reduced / Critical Severe arterial insufficiency; high risk of Critical Limb-Threatening Ischemia (CLTI) and ischemic ulceration. Urgent vascular surgical evaluation, wound care protocols, angiography, revascularization consideration.

When is a Toe-Brachial Index Clinically Required?

Under national clinical guidelines, an ABI test is not sufficient as a sole screening modality in several specific patient cohorts:

  • Whenever the ABI Exceeds 1.40: Readings above 1.40 designate incompressible tibial arteries (Mönckeberg's medial calcific sclerosis). Because the pressure cannot collapse the vessel, the ABI score is rendered technically invalid. The TBI must be measured immediately as the authoritative backup.
  • Patients with Longstanding Diabetes Mellitus: Diabetic patients frequently harbor extensive medial artery calcification alongside sensory neuropathy. In many diabetic patients, the ABI is falsely reported as "normal" (e.g., 1.05) even while severe infrapopliteal occlusion starves the toes of oxygen. A TBI should be performed routinely for diabetic foot risk stratifications.
  • End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) and Hemodialysis: Secondary hyperparathyroidism and abnormal calcium-phosphate metabolism lead to intense vascular calcification. In dialysis patients, the TBI is vastly more reliable than the ABI for predicting ulcer healing and limb salvage.
  • Non-Healing Foot Ulcers or Gangrene: When assessing the wound-healing capacity of a diabetic foot ulcer, an absolute toe systolic pressure of at least 30 mmHg (or ideally > 50 mmHg) is necessary for spontaneous tissue healing without revascularization.

How Toe Pressure is Measured in the Vascular Lab

Unlike arm and ankle pressures which utilize standard acoustic Doppler probes, toe systolic pressure is obtained through specialized equipment:

  1. A miniature pneumatic digit cuff (typically 1.5 cm to 2.5 cm in width) is positioned snugly around the proximal phalanx of the great toe (hallux).
  2. A photoplethysmography (PPG) infrared sensor is taped to the plantar surface of the toe pad. The PPG device emits infrared light that reflects off red blood cells in the microvascular capillary bed, displaying continuous pulsatile waveforms on a digital monitor.
  3. The cuff is inflated until the pulsatile waveform flattens completely (occlusion), usually 20–30 mmHg above systemic systolic pressure.
  4. The technician gradually deflates the toe cuff at a controlled rate of 2 mmHg per second. The exact manometer pressure at which the first arterial pulse waveform reappears is recorded as the toe systolic pressure.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Toe-Brachial Index (TBI)? ↓

The toe-brachial index (TBI) is an objective, non-invasive physiological test that compares systolic blood pressure measured at the great toe to the highest systolic pressure measured in the upper arms. It detects peripheral arterial disease in small distal vessels when ankle arteries cannot be occluded.

What is a normal Toe-Brachial Index (TBI) value? ↓

Under American Heart Association (AHA) and Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) guidelines, a TBI greater than 0.70 is normal. Values between 0.41 and 0.70 indicate mild-to-moderate arterial obstruction, and a TBI of 0.40 or below reflects critical limb ischemia.

Why is TBI preferred over ABI in diabetes and kidney disease? ↓

Patients with diabetes or chronic kidney disease (CKD) frequently develop Mönckeberg's medial sclerosis, causing stiffening and calcification of the tibial arteries at the ankle. This prevents cuff occlusion and creates a falsely elevated ABI (> 1.40). Digital arteries in the toes lack a muscular tunica media and rarely calcify, preserving accurate pressure readings.

How is toe blood pressure measured clinically? ↓

Toe blood pressure is measured using a miniature 1.5 cm to 2.5 cm pneumatic cuff placed around the base of the great toe (hallux), combined with a photoplethysmography (PPG) infrared sensor on the toe pad to detect the return of arterial pulsation upon cuff deflation.
